# Second Opinion: Healthcare analysis

**Table:** Second Opinion
**Industry:** Healthcare
**Approach(es):** Simulation (setup only), Forecasting
**Date:** 2026-10-16
**Horizon:** Three to five years (2030)

## Summary

We imagined a 2030 in which everyone has instant, unlimited access to an AI that can make the knowledge-based decisions doctors make today, and asked what happens to health services. Our gut call was that AI-first visits will have mostly happened and that clinic capacity will rise a lot. Our draft view is that the AI's skill is not the only gate: payment rules, legal accountability, and patient trust decide the pace. Even with AI doing the thinking, hands-on care, explaining plans, and consent still need people, and these could become the new bottleneck. Spare clinician time does not automatically mean lower spending or fewer jobs. Insurers and biotech follow from whatever services change.

## 1. The change we investigated
By 2030, most first visits are handled by AI, with a nurse or doctor only for hands-on care (our scenario assumption, not a fact). Our initial expectation was that this has mostly happened and that patient capacity rises a lot.

## 2. What we found happening already
No direct healthcare evidence was collected in this session. As a cross-sector caution only, METR's July 10, 2025 study ([report](https://metr.org/blog/2025-07-10-early-2025-ai-experienced-os-dev-study/)) found that experienced developers' sense of speed differed from measured results. This is a prepared summary, not rechecked live, and says nothing about clinics.

## 3. A plausible future and its assumptions
*Conditional scenarios proposed by Claude and accepted by the table without revision; not probabilities.*

| 2030 | Faster change | Slower change |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Work | AI handles intake, diagnosis, and plan; staff do exams, procedures, and explanation | AI drafts; a clinician signs off every decision |
| Must be true | Insurers pay for AI-led visits; clear legal accountability; patients trust it | Liability stays with clinicians; payment requires human sign-off |
| Gains / costs | Patients gain access; clinics retrain staff; errors may scale quickly | Safer pacing; smaller access gains; clinician time stays the bottleneck |

Separating assumption: **who is accountable and paid when the AI decides.** Causal chain: AI decision task → visits redesigned around hands-on care → insurers change what they pay for → biotech markets evidence to AI systems.

Signposts (thresholds are table hypotheses): (1) whether CMS's annual Physician Fee Schedule or major insurers pay for an AI-led visit without physician sign-off, which supports the faster future; (2) published clinician override and error rates from health systems running AI-first triage, where rising or high overrides support the slower future.

Robust action for either future: train staff to check AI decisions and explain them to patients, and track override rates.

The simulation was set up with fictional clinic inputs but stopped before results were shown, so no numbers are reported.

## 4. What people and organizations would still contribute
Hands-on care, explaining options, consent, and accountability for outcomes. For a new graduate: skill in checking AI decisions and communicating with patients may matter more.

## 5. Our biggest disagreement or uncertainty
Main uncertainty: whether payment rules and legal accountability change as fast as AI capability. Table disagreement: not collected.
