# JACSS: Healthcare Access analysis

**Table:** JACSS
**Industry:** Healthcare Access
**Approach(es):** Simulation, with brief live research
**Date:** 2026-10-16
**Horizon:** Three to five years

## Summary

We explored whether AI agents, with a clinician reviewing every recommendation, could route rural and after-hours patients to the right level of care. In our fictional model of 100 callers, correctly routed patients rose from 51 to 90. Most of that gain came from answering everyone, not from the agent being smarter. When we cut demand to a more realistic 15 callers, the gain nearly disappeared. Studies in Australia and Portugal suggest AI-plus-nurse triage is already in use, mostly after hours, but we found no rural U.S. evaluation. Clinicians remain essential for review, overrides, and trust. Our biggest uncertainty is whether rural areas lack a staffed line at all.

## 1. The change we investigated
Agents gather symptoms after hours and recommend self-care, telehealth, urgent care, or the ER, with a clinician approving each recommendation. Our initial expectation was that this would improve access **a lot**.

## 2. What we found happening already
- **Census Bureau, "America: A Nation of Small Towns" (May 2020):** about 76% of roughly 19,500 incorporated places have fewer than 5,000 people. https://census.gov/library/stories/2020/05/america-a-nation-of-small-towns.html
- **Healthdirect Australia (reported by The Medical Republic; date not checked):** nurses agreed with AI care-level recommendations in 83% of cases, and about 69% of encounters were after hours. Vendor co-authored. https://www.medicalrepublic.com.au/ai-triage-works-sending-people-to-the-right-care-healthdirect-finds/123169
- **Médis, Portugal (vendor summary, 2024):** 54,587 encounters; AI plus nurse triage improved care-level alignment, especially after hours. https://contact.infermedica.com/hubfs/publications/Medis-NAL-2024_one-pager.pdf

We found no rural-specific or independent U.S. evaluation. We also found no source for after-hours call volumes.

## 3. A plausible future and its assumptions
**Fictional model:** 100 callers per night. Today, the nurse line answers 60 and routes 85% of them correctly (51 patients). With agent intake, everyone is answered: the agent routes 80% correctly and the clinician catches half of its errors (90 patients). At 15 callers, the results are about 13 versus 13–14. This assumes clinicians have enough time to review every case, that patients follow the advice, and that the internet is reliable. The plan fails if the review queue backs up or if the agent misses an emergency. **Slower alternative:** worries about liability keep these tools in daytime clinics only. **Sign to watch:** whether rural health systems start after-hours AI triage lines.

## 4. What people and organizations would still contribute
Clinicians approve recommendations, override them, and take responsibility for the decision. They also earn patients' trust. A new graduate might work as a triage reviewer, checking agent summaries.

## 5. Our biggest disagreement or uncertainty
Does a rural area lack a night line altogether, or does it lack capacity on the line it has? A small test would be to count after-hours calls and ER visits in one rural county.

## 6. What changed in our thinking
Before: improve access a lot. Now: we still think AI will change access a lot, though we learned the gain depends mostly on answering calls that would otherwise go unanswered, which in turn depends on caller volume.

Report-out: We explored AI-assisted after-hours triage for rural patients. We were surprised by how much the result depends on the number of callers. Our biggest uncertainty is whether rural areas lack a line altogether or just capacity.
