# If Claude is unavailable

Choose any one of the five approaches below: research, simulation, role playing, forecasting, or problem solving. You can switch if another question becomes more useful; keep the same clock. One person keeps time. Another writes. Use the case, evidence cards, and other resources in the backup pack. The examples are fictional; the source cards describe real studies with limits. Every exercise ends with the same six-part brief and a succinct one-paragraph summary. Name your table, then ask the student to save and push the reviewed report using `docs/report-out.md` when access is available.

## Research: is it happening?

- **0–2:** Choose one claim. Say what you expect. Example: “Agents could help our firm serve more clients.”
- **2–7:** Read evidence cards E1 and E2. For each, name what it found and what it cannot tell us about our firm.
- **7–12:** Ask: Which study is more relevant to our task? What important evidence is missing? Do the differing results necessarily contradict each other?
- **12–16:** Revise the claim. Say what might change for the service or customers. What must people still do? What changed in your thinking?
- **16–20:** Fill in the six-part worksheet. Cite the cards' original sources and mark them “prepared summaries; not rechecked live.” Preserve disagreement.

If you use another industry, the cards may be analogies only. “We need industry evidence” is a useful finding. This is a short evidence scan, not a comprehensive review.

## Simulation: what changes?

- **0–2:** Ask whether the firm could finish more briefs. Say what you expect.
- **2–6:** Read the case and worked numbers. Challenge one assumption.
- **6–11:** Compare 12 briefs today with eight under the agent assumptions. Pick one input to change. Recalculate using the smaller of analyst capacity, review capacity, and demand; round down. Use the worked table if you prefer.
- **11–16:** Ask what limits completed work. What costs, quality issues, or customer needs are missing? What might change in the service or competitive landscape? What changed in your thinking?
- **16–20:** Fill in the worksheet. Label the numbers fictional. Include the changed assumption and a measurement that would test it.

Changing only agent review time to 0.75 hours gives 16 completed briefs in the prepared model. This does not prove that quality stays equal or that clients will buy them.

## Role playing: who sees it differently?

- **0–2:** Decide whether the firm should pilot a brief service for smaller clients. Say your initial view.
- **2–5:** Choose three or four role cards. Assign roles to willing participants.
- **5–10:** Each person gives one need and one worry in about 20 seconds. Which conflict matters most?
- **10–14:** Add this fictional condition: clients require traceable sources for every important claim. Revise the pilot, or explain why you would decline it.
- **14–16:** Step out of character. What did we assume about these people? Who would we need to ask? What changed in our thinking?
- **16–20:** Fill in the worksheet. Include the conflict, revised idea, and one real stakeholder question.

Imagined reactions are hypotheses. They are not survey findings or evidence that customers would buy the service.

## Forecasting: what could make change faster or slower?

- **0–2:** Pick a concrete change by 2030. Say what you currently expect.
- **2–6:** Choose one uncertain driver: review quality, data access, customer trust, or implementation capacity.
- **6–11:** Describe a faster-change future and a slower-change future. For each: what happens to the work, what must be true, and what people still contribute?
- **11–14:** Name two signs you could actually observe. Say what to measure, where to get it, and when to check. Example: completed briefs at unchanged quality during a documented pilot.
- **14–16:** Pick an action useful in both futures. What changed in your thinking?
- **16–20:** Fill in the worksheet. Include both futures, the separating assumption, and the signs to watch.

These are possible paths, not calibrated predictions. Any proposed threshold is an assumption to discuss, not an industry benchmark.

## Problem solving: what could stop this from working?

- **0–2:** Define success. Say what you expect. Example: more completed briefs at equal quality.
- **2–6:** Pick the main obstacle. In the prepared model, review is limiting; the reason for slow review remains untested.
- **6–10:** Compare three responses: improve source packets, narrow the service, or keep the current process. Choose one to test.
- **10–14:** Pick one constraint card. Which response survives? What needs to change?
- **14–16:** Design a small test: responsible role, scope, comparison, result to measure, quality check, and stop/continue rule. What changed in your thinking?
- **16–20:** Fill in the worksheet. Record the response, constraint, test, and uncertainty.

This is a test plan. You have not yet shown that the response works. Do not treat a plausible explanation as a proven root cause.
