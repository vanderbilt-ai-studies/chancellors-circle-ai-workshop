# Role playing: who sees this differently?

Read `docs/facilitation.md` first. It sets the one-question-at-a-time interaction, evidence labels, switching behavior, and four-minute wrap-up. All paths below are relative to the repository root. Use the supplied topic and time; ask only for what is missing. Ask for the table's initial expectation before doing analysis. If no topic is chosen, offer `materials/advisory-firm-case.md` and wait for agreement.

The student keeps time. The schedule below totals 20 minutes including a first brief. At four minutes remaining, skip unfinished exploration and draft. Do not run all the steps in one answer.

## 0–2 minutes: name a decision

Turn the proposal into a choice: “Should this organization pilot this service?” Ask the table to confirm or change it. Do not try to role-play an entire industry.

## 2–5 minutes: choose three or four roles

Read `materials/role-cards.md`. Offer roles with distinct goals; include someone affected and someone accountable. Ask people to choose roles and change unrealistic interests. Describe each role in one sentence. Avoid real-person impersonation and demographic stereotypes. Claude may play one missing role; the humans should do most of the talking. For another industry, adapt the roles to the same concrete decision.

## 5–10 minutes: hear the conflict

Ask each participant, one at a time: “What do you need, and what worries you?” Suggest 20 seconds each. Summarize the competing interests in at most four bullets. If Claude plays a role, label its two-sentence contribution “imagined perspective.” Do not generate a long dialogue or suggest that a majority of fictional roles predicts acceptance.

Ask: “Which conflict matters most for this decision?” Wait. Preserve the conflict rather than resolving it with a generic promise that everyone benefits.

## 10–14 minutes: change one condition

Offer one relevant fictional change, such as “clients now require traceable sources for every claim.” Ask how the table would revise the pilot. Each person can name one acceptable condition or unresolved objection. Explicitly distinguish the role's incentives from the participant's own view.

## 14–16 minutes: step out of role

Say: “Let's step out of character.” Ask: “What did we assume about these people that we should check with them?” Identify one real question for a real stakeholder, without contacting anyone. Ask what changed in the table's thinking. Treat every imagined reaction as a hypothesis, never as survey or interview evidence.

## 16–20 minutes: write the brief

Use `templates/scenario-brief.md`. Include the decision, central conflict, revised proposal, remaining objection, human contribution, and a stakeholder question to investigate. The already-happening section needs genuine sources or an explicit evidence gap. Do not cite the role-play as proof of acceptance or resistance.

Collect the table's ideas into a succinct one-paragraph summary and review it together. Ask the table to name itself. Follow `docs/report-out.md` to save `report-out/[table-name]-[industry]-analysis.md`; prompt the student to provide the chosen name and ask for the reviewed results to be committed and pushed. Publish only after that request, and return the verified report link.

## Basis

A shortened adaptation of IDEO.org's Role Play, with a reflection step informed by AHRQ debriefing. The original activity generally takes longer; AI-generated roles are an additional workshop device. See `docs/methods-and-sources.md`.
