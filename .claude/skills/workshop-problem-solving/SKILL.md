# Problem solving: what gets in the way?

Read `docs/facilitation.md` first. It sets the one-question-at-a-time interaction, evidence labels, switching behavior, and four-minute wrap-up. All paths below are relative to the repository root. Use the supplied topic and time; ask only for what is missing. Ask for the table's initial expectation before doing analysis. If no topic is chosen, offer `materials/advisory-firm-case.md` and wait for agreement.

The student keeps time. The schedule below totals 20 minutes including a first brief. At four minutes remaining, skip unfinished exploration and draft. Do not run all the steps in one answer.

## 0–2 minutes: define success

Confirm the proposed future. Ask what observable result would make it worthwhile. For the prepared case, success might be more completed briefs at the same verified quality, not simply faster drafts.

## 2–6 minutes: choose one obstacle

Offer up to three plausible obstacles and ask which most threatens success. Separate an observed problem from a suspected cause. In the prepared model, reviewer capacity limits output under the assumptions; why review takes that long is untested. Repeated “why” questions do not establish causality. Form a narrow challenge: “How could we ___ while preserving ___?”

## 6–10 minutes: compare responses

Offer three materially different responses, including a modest process change or keeping the current process. Example: improve source packets, narrow the service, or postpone expansion. Show a small table: response | why it might help | main tradeoff | what must be true. Ask which deserves a test and why. Avoid invented precision in scoring or automatically selecting the most technological option.

## 10–14 minutes: introduce a constraint

Read `materials/constraint-cards.md`. Ask the table to choose one, or offer the first. Apply it to the selected response and a credible alternative. Ask what still works and what needs changing. A paper walkthrough or worked simulation can expose a flaw; label it a rehearsal, not real-world implementation or measured validation.

## 14–16 minutes: design a small test

Offer a short pilot plan: a responsible role, a small scope, comparison with the current process, one outcome measure, a quality check, and a stop/continue rule. Ask what would make the test informative. Proposed sample sizes and thresholds are workshop hypotheses, not claims of statistical adequacy. For the prepared case, suggest a few public-information briefs reviewed against a common checklist with time recorded; do not run a real client pilot.

## 16–20 minutes: write the brief

Ask what changed in the table's thinking, then use `templates/scenario-brief.md`. Capture the obstacle, chosen response and alternative, new constraint, revised plan, and small test. State what remains untested. Include a genuine current finding with a source or an explicit evidence gap. Do not claim the obstacle has been solved because a plan exists.

Collect the table's ideas into a succinct one-paragraph summary and review it together. Ask the table to name itself. Follow `docs/report-out.md` to save `report-out/[table-name]-[industry]-analysis.md`; prompt the student to provide the chosen name and ask for the reviewed results to be committed and pushed. Publish only after that request, and return the verified report link.

## Basis

Informed by ASQ's Plan-Do-Check-Act cycle and GO-Science policy stress-testing. This exercise does the planning and a paper challenge; actual doing, checking, and acting would happen after a real authorized test. See `docs/methods-and-sources.md`.
